# The conversion brief, the page structure, and the copy

A landing page is one argument aimed at one visitor, ending in one action. This file
covers how to extract that argument from a brief, how the standard section sequence
serves it, and how to write the copy so it reads like a marketer wrote it.

## 1. Interrogating the brief

Work through these before any design decision. Where the brief does not answer one, ask
the user rather than inventing an answer.

**Who is the visitor, and what do they already believe?**
Not a demographic, a state of mind. What are they doing right now that this replaces?
What have they already tried? What do they suspect is true about this category, right or
wrong? A page written for someone who has never heard of the category and a page written
for someone comparing three vendors are different pages.

**How aware are they?** Roughly five stages, and the hero changes completely across them:

| Stage | They know | The hero must lead with |
|---|---|---|
| Unaware | nothing, not even that they have the problem | the symptom, named in their words |
| Problem-aware | the pain, not the solutions | the problem, then that a solution exists |
| Solution-aware | the category, not you | why this approach beats the alternative |
| Product-aware | you, but not convinced | the differentiator and the proof |
| Most aware | you, and they are ready | the offer and the ask |

Most pages fail by writing for "most aware" visitors when the traffic is problem-aware.

**What is the one action?** Buy, book, start a trial, request a demo, join a list. Every
section either moves toward it or is cut. Secondary actions (a pricing link, a FAQ jump)
are allowed as support, never as a second co-equal CTA competing in the hero.

**What is the promise?** The outcome, in the visitor's language. "Your Monday report
done before lunch" is a promise. "AI-powered productivity tooling" is a category label.
The promise must be specific enough to be falsifiable.

**What is the mechanism?** Why the promise is believable. Buyers discount promises they
cannot explain to themselves. The mechanism is usually what the "how it works" or "why
us" section exists to carry.

**What proof genuinely exists?** Inventory it honestly:
- track record, numbers, named customers, ratings (if real),
- credentials and relevant history of whoever is behind it,
- artifacts: what the visitor leaves with, sample output, a demo, a real screenshot,
- transparency: showing the actual curriculum, the actual pricing, the actual process,
- risk reversal: guarantee, refund, credit, cancellation terms, free first step.

Pre-launch products have the last four and none of the first. That is a real constraint
on the evidence section, not a licence to fabricate. See "Evidence when there is no
track record" below.

**What are the objections?** List the top three to five in the visitor's own words
("I'm not technical enough," "my team will never adopt it," "what if it doesn't work for
our setup," "this looks expensive"). Each one needs a home: defused in a section, or
answered plainly in the FAQ.

**What is the commitment and what reverses the risk?** Price, time, effort, what happens
immediately after they act, and what protects them if it goes wrong.

## 2. The section sequence, and what each one is for

Default order. Deviate deliberately and say why.

**Header.** Product name, minimal nav, a persistent CTA. It is not a site menu; it is a
rail back to the action.

**Hero.** The promise, who it is for, the primary CTA, and (when relevant) one line of
proof or a risk-reversal note under the button. Include the single strongest supporting
visual. Keep it to one idea. A hero that lists five benefits communicates none.

**Outcome / benefit.** Turn the promise into concrete, checkable specifics: what the
visitor can do afterwards that they cannot do now, or what artifact they walk away with.
Describe results, not features. Three to five items, each specific enough that a
sceptical reader can picture it.

**Evidence.** The believability section. Use the strongest proof that actually exists,
in this order of persuasive power when available: results from named real customers >
third-party validation > credentials and track record of the people behind it >
transparency about the actual process or contents > artifacts and samples. Quotes work
hardest when they name the specific objection they overcame.

**The offer.** The actual thing being bought, with every detail a decision needs: price,
what is included, availability, variants, dates, delivery mode, capacity. This section
is where money changes hands and is chronically under-designed relative to the hero.
Make state visible and honest: sold out, only N left, price after a discount someone
qualifies for. If a personalised price exists (member, alumni, annual billing), show how
it is obtained rather than hiding it until checkout.

**Why us / how it works.** The mechanism, and the reduction of process risk: what
happens step by step, in the product's real sequence from the brief. If the real flow is
five steps, show five. Never a decorative "Discover / Engage / Grow" triptych.

**FAQ.** The objection list from Step 1, phrased the way the visitor would phrase it,
answered truthfully against the product's real rules. An FAQ that only asks flattering
questions convinces nobody; include the awkward ones (cancellation, refunds, whether it
suits their specific situation).

**Closing CTA.** For the reader who scrolled everything and is still deciding. Restate
the promise in one line and make the next step concrete and immediate: name the actual
next available date, the actual entry plan, the actual first thing that happens. Do not
repeat the hero CTA copy verbatim.

**Footer.** Practical, honest, and quiet: contact, legal, secondary links.

## 3. Writing the copy

The copy is the conversion mechanism. Design amplifies it; it cannot substitute for it.

**Specific beats vague.** Every abstraction is a place the reader stops believing.
"Automate your reporting" is weaker than "turn the CSV you rebuild every Friday into one
command you run yourself."

**Use their language.** Pull nouns and verbs from the brief's description of the
customer's world, not from the product's internal vocabulary.

**Benefit before feature.** Lead with what changes for them; name the feature second as
the reason it is possible.

**Say true things.** Never write a number, a claim, or a testimonial that is not backed
by the brief. Unverifiable superlatives ("the leading platform") read as noise to
sceptical buyers and are indistinguishable from filler.

**Headline and lead length.** Headlines at or under about 12 words; section leads and
hero subheads at or under about 25 to 30 words. Longer than that and the reader skims
past the point. If the idea will not fit, the idea is not sharp enough yet.

**One idea per section.** If a section is making two arguments, it is two sections or
one of them is cut.

**Voice: a marketer, not an AI assistant.** The specific tells to avoid are in
`anti-slop-and-tone.md` and they matter as much as any visual guardrail: no em dashes,
no "not just X, it's Y," no reflexive "actually / real / genuinely" as authenticity
filler, no consultant vocabulary, no signposting. Vary sentence length. Allow a plain
declarative to land without softening it.

**Microcopy is copy.** Button labels, form hints, empty states, the line under the CTA,
the sold-out badge: these carry more conversion weight per word than body copy. "Book a
seat" beats "Submit." A reassurance under a button ("no account needed, pay in one step")
removes more friction than another paragraph above it.

## 4. Evidence when there is no track record

Common for pre-launch products, and the moment most pages start lying. Never invent
customer counts, ratings, logos, or testimonials presented as real. Instead:

- **Credibility of the person or team**: specific and checkable ("six years building
  internal automation tooling at a logistics company"), never "expert team."
- **Transparency as proof**: publish the actual contents, the actual agenda, the actual
  method. Showing the real thing is itself evidence.
- **Artifacts**: what the visitor ends up holding, with a sample or a screenshot.
- **Demonstration**: show the product doing the thing, rather than claiming it.
- **Risk reversal**: a clear, generous, plainly stated guarantee or cancellation term
  does more for a cold audience than a fabricated statistic would.
- **Illustrative placeholder content** (a sample quote, sample pricing) is acceptable in
  a design comp the same way lorem-adjacent sample product names are, as long as it is
  understood as placeholder and would be replaced by real content before launch. It must
  never be a fabricated factual claim dressed as real.
